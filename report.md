## Latency isolation follow-up: matched paths and kernel observations

The follow-up isolates a measurable marginal cost for each custom tunnel while holding its VM attachment and cluster configuration fixed. All five paired differences were positive: the custom VM path added **224 ±47 µs**, and the custom host path added **257 ±118 µs**, to mean TCP request/response latency. These are round-trip transaction differences, not one-way wire delays or an intrinsic cost of writing a VXLAN header. Our scripts configure Linux VXLAN devices; they do not implement a separate userspace packet encoder. Cilium and both custom deployments use the Linux VXLAN routines, with different forwarding integration and metadata.

| Controlled block, five pairs | Bypass mean µs ± SD | Tunnel mean µs ± SD | Paired additional mean µs ± SD |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Custom VM VXLAN | 721.7 ±45.7 | 946.2 ±42.9 | 224.5 ±46.8 |
| Custom host VXLAN | 756.8 ±129.9 | 1013.6 ±14.6 | 256.8 ±117.6 |

The VM control alternated remote PodCIDR routes between the existing tunnel and direct next hops without recreating the tunnel, socket, static neighbors, or FDB. The host control alternated the same bridge between its VXLAN port and physical uplink, retaining the same guest TAP interfaces. Balanced tunnel/bypass order, unchanged VM boots, fixed queue counts and CPU masks, and live route/bridge validation remove important confounds from the earlier cross-mode comparison. Each observation used a separate 30-second Cilium CLI TCP_RR test with diagnostics. CPU placement remained automatic within the fixed masks, and fresh benchmark Pods/connections can change RSS placement. The differences therefore represent the added complete tunnel path under this configuration, including its effects on batching, scheduling and interrupts.

The narrow header routines were much smaller than the measured transaction differences. In separate instrumented runs, the VXLAN build-SKB helper averaged approximately **3.1 µs per call for Cilium**, **3.4–3.5 µs for our VM tunnel**, and **3.3 µs for our host tunnel**; header-pull means were similarly a few microseconds. These routines include preparation and offload handling, not just byte writes. The observed values include probe overhead and cannot establish an exact uninstrumented header cost. They provide no evidence for a custom header-building algorithm costing hundreds of microseconds. The fresh, separately configured Cilium reference measured 762 ±62 µs mean transaction latency; it is not a route-only paired control against the native conditions above.

The clearest implementation distinction is the forwarding path around the tunnel. In the instrumented VM-tunnel run, the Pod-TCP `ip_forward` probe recorded approximately 21,000 calls on each guest during the tracing window; the same available probe recorded zero calls under Cilium VXLAN. Attachment snapshots showed **generic native `cil_from_netdev` TCX ingress on our `cs740vx` device**, while Cilium's `cilium_vxlan` had **dedicated `cil_from_overlay` ingress and `cil_to_overlay` egress programs**. Thus, both paths use Cilium BPF, but the observed custom native-plus-tunnel configuration traverses ordinary Linux forwarding stages that the integrated Cilium overlay avoids for this traffic. Our VM `ip_forward` callback means rose from roughly 34–37 µs in bypass to 65–66 µs with the tunnel, including nested transmission work. This supports an integration/forwarding penalty rather than a large difference in the common header helper, without assigning the entire Cilium/custom latency gap to one function.

For the host tunnel, the extra work moved into the hypervisor backend. Instrumented worker `handle_tx` means were about **108–110 µs with host VXLAN versus 52–55 µs in bridge bypass**. The full VXLAN transmit callback averaged about 57 µs and included roughly 35 µs inside outer UDP/IP transmission; this is broader work than header preparation and includes downstream processing. Receive callbacks and the matched receive-to-GRO handoff expose additional stages. Scheduler wakeup-to-run means for the worker vCPUs were around 9–12 µs and changed little across conditions, so the traces do not demonstrate that generic runnable scheduler delay explains the whole penalty. They also do not cover every hardware-interrupt or virtual-interrupt delivery delay. The observations locate additional synchronous networking/backend work, but their inclusive durations overlap and cannot be summed into an RTT budget.

A separate three-round, four-condition host experiment tested CPU idle-policy interaction without tracing. It compared both paths under the default policy and under descriptor-scoped 10 µs CPU latency QoS requests on both hypervisors, retaining the same VM boots, queues and masks throughout that block. The request constrains permitted CPU idle-state latency; it is not an application-latency guarantee. Idle-state counters verified that states advertising latency above 10 µs were not entered during constrained cases, and the original effective policy was restored after every case.

| Default policy | 401.3 ±211.6 | 975.8 ±34.0 | 574.5 ±214.6 |
| 10 µs CPU latency QoS | 196.0 ±36.4 | 494.3 ±19.1 | 298.3 ±23.0 |

The policy reduced the measured marginal penalty in all three rounds, by 63–472 µs, with a mean interaction of 276 ±205 µs. This is exploratory evidence that host idle policy amplifies the apparent tunnel cost; the small sample and broad default-policy variation prevent a precise universal allocation to idle exits. The substantial remaining 298 µs penalty shows that suppressing deep idle does not remove the added tunnel path's cost. These fresh defaults must be analyzed within their own block rather than pooled with the earlier five-pair baseline. No governor or affinity setting was changed, no throughput claim follows from this latency test, and power consumption was not measured. [Linux documents the descriptor-scoped CPU latency QoS mechanism](https://docs.kernel.org/power/pm_qos_interface.html).

Each instrumented 30-second case had only about 15 seconds of active tracing; its overall mean therefore dilutes a substantial tracing-window perturbation. Function timing is useful for identifying executed stages and their scale, not for an exact additive CPU budget. ## How the suite operates

The test suite compares native routing and VXLAN networking across Kubernetes virtual machines on two CloudLab hypervisors. The collector, `experiments/collect.py`, coordinates infrastructure preparation, cluster setup, measurement, and result storage. Before each experiment mode, it resets Kubernetes, reboots the guests, reconciles their network attachments and MTUs, and rebuilds the cluster with the selected Cilium configuration. Existing VM disks, management connectivity, and SSH keys are preserved. The runner validates endpoint placement, node readiness, and Cilium routing and MTU settings before measuring traffic. Cross-hypervisor experiments reuse the same worker pair, normally worker-1 and worker-2, while the local baseline uses two workers on one hypervisor. Defaults are five samples, 30 seconds per test and sample, four parallel streams for multistream throughput, and one collection round. The collector holds the chosen host tuning profile constant across modes, audits NIC settings, and can repeat rounds with the mode order reversed on alternating rounds. Progress and failures are recorded in a collection manifest, and interrupted collections can resume without overwriting successful measurements.

Three modes establish the routing and virtualization baselines. The `native` experiment uses Cilium native routing with a 1500-byte Pod MTU and no VXLAN encapsulation. The `direct-1450` experiment uses the same routing approach with a 1450-byte Pod MTU, while retaining a 1500-byte guest transport interface. Comparing these modes tests whether reducing the Pod packet size changes performance without adding a tunnel. The `vm-local` experiment measures host-network traffic between two distinct VMs on the same physical hypervisor at MTU 1500. It bypasses the Pod forwarding path and provides a baseline for communication through the local VM networking infrastructure. Diagnostic mode verifies same-host placement with a separate five-second multistream run, an egress-only physical-interface capture, live bridge-mode macvtap attachment checks, and physical NIC transmit-byte counters. Linux exposes local macvtap forwarding to the lower device's capture taps, so an ordinary bidirectional tcpdump capture cannot establish wire transmission. The validation requires no captured benchmark egress or capture drops and bounds total physical transmit bytes below 1% of estimated application bytes, including background traffic. In the suite's terminology, host-network traffic runs in the Kubernetes VM's network namespace; it does not mean the benchmark runs directly on the physical hypervisor.

Five modes compare overlay implementations and tunnel placement. The `vxlan` mode uses Cilium-managed VXLAN inside each VM, with a 1450-byte Pod MTU over a 1500-byte transport network. The `vm-vxlan` mode uses manually configured Linux VXLAN devices and forwarding inside each VM, with a 1500-byte inner MTU and 1550-byte guest and physical transport MTUs. The `host-vxlan` mode moves the custom tunnel to a shared Linux bridge and VXLAN device on each hypervisor, retaining a 1500-byte inner MTU and using a 1550-byte physical transport MTU. The additional `vm-vxlan-1450` and `host-vxlan-1450` modes use a 1450-byte inner MTU over a 1500-byte physical network, allowing comparison with Cilium VXLAN at equal inner packet size. Guest-based encapsulation still requires a 1500-byte guest transport interface to accommodate the added headers; with host-based encapsulation, the guest interface can use MTU 1450. The matched three-implementation comparison uses Pod-only traffic because host-network controls bypass guest tunnels but traverse the shared hypervisor tunnel. These experiments compare complete forwarding paths, including their bridge and interface arrangements, rather than isolating the cost of header creation alone. Cilium uses UDP port 8472 and the custom tunnels use port 4789, another difference that can affect flow distribution.

Performance measurements use the controller's installed Cilium CLI through `cilium connectivity perf`, which runs netperf workloads on the selected workers. TCP request/response (`TCP_RR`) measures the time to complete a request and response, reporting minimum, mean, maximum, P50, P90, and P99 latency in the console, together with transactions per second. These are transaction latencies rather than one-way network delays. Single-stream throughput (`TCP_STREAM`) measures the bandwidth achieved by one TCP connection, while multistream throughput (`TCP_STREAM_MULTI`) measures aggregate bandwidth across the configured parallel connections. Comparing the two helps identify whether a single flow falls short of the capacity available to multiple flows. Except for the local baseline or an explicit Pod-only selection, measurements include both Pod-to-Pod and host-network paths. The ordinary runner combines the test cases in one invocation; adding `--diagnostics` executes each sample, network path, and test type separately. This separation supports phase-specific telemetry and preserves an individual Cilium JSON report for each sample. Five samples of three test types across two network paths represent 15 minutes of benchmark traffic per mode, with additional time for setup, snapshots, and cleanup.

The ordinary runner saves aggregate whole-invocation CPU snapshots, while diagnostic mode starts collectors on both endpoint VMs and their hypervisors before each benchmark invocation. Approximately once per second, collectors record per-core CPU counters, softirq and interrupt counts, network backlog counters, TCP/IP counters, interface traffic counters, socket details, and visible netperf process IDs and commands. Derived CPU metrics include busy time, softirq time, and guest steal time, which helps reveal when a VM is waiting for physical CPU scheduling. TCP socket observations expose available congestion-window and retransmission information, and network backlog counters show packet drops and processing-budget exhaustion. Before and after each invocation, the collectors also save NIC and queue statistics, checksum and segmentation offload settings, channel counts, receive-side queue indirection, interrupt affinity, software receive/transmit steering masks, interface details, and routes. Unsupported queries and missing tools are recorded explicitly. An optional, separate `--profile` diagnostic run uses system-wide `perf` sampling on the guests and hypervisors to identify kernel functions consuming CPU; this requires an existing usable perf installation and is kept separate because profiling can alter performance.

Results are stored under a unique directory in `results/`, including original Cilium JSON reports, console logs, endpoint and Pod placement, live Cilium configuration, commands, timestamps, and success or failure metadata. The suite exports reported performance values to `metrics.csv`; diagnostic runs additionally provide `diagnostics.csv`, per-phase `summary.json` files, and raw timestamped `samples.jsonl` records for each monitored machine. Interpretation must account for several limits. CPU and TCP summaries use an interior window selected from observed client netperf activity, with approximate alignment based on when samples arrive over SSH. NIC and queue deltas cover the entire invocation, including setup and cleanup. The extended diagnostic runner discovers benchmark Pod UIDs from Kubernetes, matches process cgroups to those UIDs, and selects workload activity only from identified benchmark netperf processes. TCP counters and data-socket observations come from those Pod network namespaces; netperf control sockets on port 12865 are excluded from data RTT summaries. Host-network Pods still share VM-wide counters. QEMU and vhost task records identify their VM domains and report individual thread CPU usage, observed CPU placement, and scheduler counters where available. Scheduler wait observations remain limited when kernel scheduler statistics are disabled, and the collectors do not account individually for every competing process. The data therefore shows resource use during a known test phase but cannot attribute all of it exclusively to VXLAN or Cilium. Monitoring itself adds overhead, so matching unmonitored controls should be retained, and repeated-sample console output should be preserved because combined Cilium JSON reports can retain only the final sample's values. Equal MTUs, consistent placement, quiet hosts, and repeated measurements support useful comparisons, while process attribution and targeted profiling remain necessary for stronger claims about the underlying bottleneck.

Potential networking overhead arises at several layers. Pod traffic passes through virtual interfaces and Cilium endpoint processing in addition to the virtual NIC and hypervisor path shared with host-network traffic. VXLAN adds tunnel forwarding, encapsulation, and decapsulation; its additional headers also reduce the payload that fits within a fixed transport MTU. Available checksum, segmentation, and receive-aggregation offloads can change how much packet processing the CPUs perform. The current baseline retains kube-proxy and does not enable the optimized eBPF host-routing profile. Regular host-stack and netfilter traversal are therefore potential costs, but retaining kube-proxy does not establish that direct Pod-IP benchmarks use Service translation or incur Service NAT. Cilium documents these host-stack and virtual-interface considerations in its [performance tuning guide](https://docs.cilium.io/en/stable/operations/performance/tuning/). Queue distribution, interrupt placement, and VM scheduling are additional candidate bottlenecks: one busy core or queue can constrain throughput while other cores remain available. The [Linux networking scaling documentation](https://docs.kernel.org/networking/scaling.html) explains how RSS, RPS, and interrupt affinity distribute packet processing. The extended study evaluates queue parallelism with one/two/restored-queue interventions and vhost affinity with unpinned/pinned/unpinned runs. The resulting evidence supports conclusions about those interventions; copy, checksum, DMA mapping, and offload interactions still require careful attribution. Moving tunnels to the hypervisor may relocate processing costs rather than eliminate them.

Measurement overhead is also part of the experiment. The remote Python samplers scan processes, read kernel counters, execute socket queries, serialize records, and stream them over SSH; encryption and collection use CPU on the monitored machines even though SSH uses management connectivity. Reading NIC statistics and writing logs add work around each invocation. Optional perf profiling adds sampling interrupts, stack collection, and trace output during the workload. None of these costs has yet been quantified for this testbed, so a fixed overhead percentage should not be assumed. Matching monitored and unmonitored runs can estimate their effect on throughput and latency; perf runs should remain a separate diagnostic category. Benchmark traffic generation itself consumes CPU, and increasing the stream count creates additional application and networking work. A high CPU reading therefore cannot be interpreted as tunnel overhead alone, and guest and hypervisor utilization should be examined together rather than simply added as independent CPU costs.

Iteration overhead affects elapsed time separately from packet-processing overhead. Cluster resets, guest reboots, image pulls, Pod readiness, SSH setup, snapshots, and cleanup extend collection beyond the configured traffic duration. Diagnostic isolation repeats much of this lifecycle for every sample, path, and test type, so reducing the number of invocations can save more time than shortening traffic alone. An exploratory comparison could use three 15-second samples of single-stream and multistream Pod traffic across the three MTU-1450 VXLAN implementations: this represents 18 invocations and 4.5 minutes of benchmark traffic, plus lifecycle overhead. This is a proposed exploratory configuration; the collector currently needs an additional workload-selection option to request both throughput types while omitting latency in one command. Short runs provide fewer diagnostic observations and give startup behavior, TCP ramp-up, and scheduling variation greater influence. They can help identify large performance differences, but small changes, steady-state claims, and tail-latency conclusions require longer runs and repeated validation. Final comparisons should retain consistent endpoints, MTUs, tuning, stream counts, and monitoring settings.

## Kernel Packet Paths and the Validity of the VXLAN Comparison

### What the experiment can isolate

The concern that the VXLAN implementations follow different paths is valid. Equal inner MTU, workload, and endpoint placement make the measurements more comparable, but they do not make the implementations differ in only one variable. The current suite measures the performance of complete deployment configurations. It can establish that one configuration delivers more throughput or lower latency in this testbed. It cannot, by itself, establish that the difference comes entirely from the VXLAN implementation, from eBPF, or from placing a tunnel on the hypervisor. For the paper, the research question should therefore concern the performance and resource distribution of these deployment choices. A claim about the isolated cost of encapsulation or management location requires additional controls.

### The shared guest and hypervisor layers

Every cross-hypervisor Pod benchmark involves two guest Linux kernels and two physical-host Linux kernels. The application uses the TCP/IP stack inside its Kubernetes VM; Cilium also runs inside that VM. Traffic then crosses a virtio network interface and its host-side backend before reaching the physical NIC. The destination performs the corresponding receive and Pod-delivery work. Our current experiment interfaces use either direct/macvtap attachment or attachment to a host Linux bridge. Both involve host networking infrastructure. However, crossing that infrastructure does not imply that the packet traverses every host IP-routing, netfilter, or TCP processing stage. A hypervisor can forward a frame without terminating its application TCP connection.

The guest NIC model and the host-side attachment answer two different questions. The model specifies what network device the guest operating system sees: in every current experiment, it sees a virtio NIC and uses the corresponding guest driver. The attachment specifies where the host-side network endpoint connects after traffic crosses the guest/host interface. Keeping virtio constant therefore holds one part of the design constant, but does not establish an identical host forwarding path. The distinction is explicit in the Terraform module: it always sets `model.type = "virtio"`, then independently selects either a direct physical-interface source or a named bridge source. Changing that source changes the host topology even if the guest still calls its NIC `experiment` and uses the same driver.

For Cilium VXLAN and custom per-VM VXLAN, the source is a physical interface with `type="direct"` and macvtap `mode="bridge"`. The host creates a macvtap endpoint associated with that interface. In this context, "direct" identifies the attachment arrangement; it does not assign the physical NIC to the VM or eliminate host software processing. The guest still exchanges traffic through its virtual NIC and a host backend. Macvtap's "bridge" mode permits direct delivery between eligible macvtap endpoints on the same host, while cross-host traffic goes toward the physical interface. It is a mode of the macvtap mechanism, not a request to create or attach to the Linux bridge `cs740br`. [Libvirt describes these macvtap modes under direct attachment](https://libvirt.org/formatdomain.html#elementsNICSDirect).

For custom hypervisor VXLAN, Terraform instead selects `type="bridge"` with `source.bridge = "cs740br"`. The guest's host-side TAP interface becomes a port of that Linux bridge, and the VXLAN device is another port. The bridge forwards Ethernet frames between these ports using its forwarding state. The remote frame then enters the host's Linux VXLAN transmit path. This is an actual additional forwarding component in the configured topology, rather than another spelling of macvtap bridge mode. [Libvirt's bridge attachment documentation](https://libvirt.org/formatdomain.html#elementsNICSBridge) describes the associated host interface and bridge connection. The exact split of work between QEMU and kernel acceleration must still be verified from the live backend configuration; the word virtio alone does not establish that split.

This matters experimentally because throughput is determined by the entire path and its bottleneck, not just by the tunnel's header-writing routine. Moving from the guest tunnel to the hypervisor tunnel simultaneously changes the tunnel's execution context, replaces macvtap attachment with a TAP/bridge path, and changes whether the virtual NIC carries an encapsulated or inner frame. Those changes may alter forwarding work, queue behavior, batching, and the effective use of offloads. A bridge is not automatically slower, nor is macvtap automatically faster: a different path can expose or remove a bottleneck elsewhere. Equal driver names, MTUs, and physical NIC settings cannot establish equal processing cost across these arrangements.

For example, if the hypervisor-tunnel configuration improves throughput, the present experiment cannot determine whether the improvement comes from executing VXLAN outside the guest, more favorable handling of inner frames at the virtual NIC boundary, or the changed host forwarding path. If it performs worse, the result likewise does not prove that sharing a host tunnel is intrinsically inefficient. The observation applies to the combined configuration. The bridge-backed native control proposed later in this section would help assess the attachment difference, and a placement comparison using a common attachment would strengthen attribution further. The paper should therefore describe both the guest NIC model and host attachment explicitly, and avoid presenting "all modes use virtio" as evidence that only tunnel placement changed.

The following paths summarize outgoing traffic. They show the important functional stages, not a verified sequence of every kernel function or hook. Receive-side decapsulation, policy processing, and delivery must also be considered when interpreting end-to-end results.

```text
Cilium VXLAN:
  Guest:      Pod TCP/IP -> veth/Cilium BPF -> Linux VXLAN -> virtio
  Hypervisor: VM backend/macvtap -> physical NIC -> network

Custom per-VM VXLAN:
  Guest:      Pod TCP/IP -> veth/Cilium BPF -> Linux routing
              -> custom Linux VXLAN -> virtio
  Hypervisor: VM backend/macvtap -> physical NIC -> network

Custom hypervisor VXLAN:
  Guest:      Pod TCP/IP -> veth/Cilium BPF -> native routing -> virtio
  Hypervisor: VM backend/TAP -> Linux bridge -> Linux VXLAN
              -> physical NIC -> network
```

### Cilium VXLAN: eBPF forwarding with a Linux tunnel

In the Cilium overlay, guest-side BPF programs participate in endpoint processing, remote-destination selection, and tunnel redirection. They supply tunnel metadata to the Linux `cilium_vxlan` device, which operates in external/metadata mode. The Linux tunnel machinery performs VXLAN transmission, and receive-side tunnel processing cooperates with Cilium's programs to deliver the inner packet. The packet is already encapsulated when it crosses the guest's virtual NIC. Thus, both Cilium BPF processing and Linux VXLAN processing contribute to this path; calling it an entirely eBPF tunnel or a kernel-bypass implementation would be inaccurate. This description is supported by the [encapsulation code for the deployed Cilium 1.20.4 release](https://github.com/cilium/cilium/blob/v1.20.4/bpf/lib/encap.h) and the project's saved external-mode VXLAN interface snapshot.

### Custom per-VM VXLAN: Linux forwarding before the tunnel

In the custom guest implementation, Cilium runs in native mode and continues to provide Pod networking and policy processing. The experiment's script installs routes for remote PodCIDRs through `cs740vx`, together with static tunnel neighbors and forwarding entries. Linux routing selects this tunnel, and the guest Linux VXLAN implementation encapsulates the packet before it reaches virtio and macvtap. The custom tunnel-selection path has no custom BPF program, but the overall Pod path still uses Cilium BPF. This is the closest existing comparison with Cilium VXLAN because both tunnel endpoints reside in the VMs and use the same class of physical attachment. Nevertheless, the preceding forwarding decisions, tunnel metadata, and receive-side handling differ. It compares Cilium-managed overlay forwarding with a manually configured native-routing-plus-overlay path, rather than comparing two interchangeable encapsulation functions.

### Custom hypervisor VXLAN: a different virtualization boundary

In the host implementation, guest Cilium uses native routing and the packet leaves the guest without VXLAN headers. The guest interface attaches to `cs740br`, and the bridge forwards remote traffic through the hypervisor's `cs740hvx` Linux VXLAN device. On the other host, Linux removes the tunnel headers before the frame crosses the destination VM's virtual NIC. Consequently, the virtio/backend boundary carries an inner frame in this mode and a tunnel frame in the guest-based modes. This can affect segmentation, checksum handling, batching, and queue selection. Tunnel work also executes in the host kernel rather than in a guest vCPU's execution context. These are plausible performance mechanisms, not confirmed benefits. The current scripts establish the paths but do not demonstrate which mechanism dominates.

This comparison changes both tunnel location and the VM attachment/forwarding arrangement. A faster host tunnel could reflect different CPU scheduling, NIC offload behavior, or a better-performing bridge/backend combination; a slower one could reflect additional bridge processing or resource contention. Guest and hypervisor CPU observations must therefore be interpreted together. Their percentages cannot simply be added, because they have different CPU-count denominators and physical-host accounting may already include time spent executing guest vCPUs. Furthermore, testing one communicating worker pair demonstrates tunnel placement for that workload, not the scaling benefit of sharing one tunnel implementation across many simultaneously active VMs.

### Controlled variables and remaining differences

| Factor | Cilium VXLAN | Custom VM VXLAN, MTU 1450 | Custom host VXLAN, MTU 1450 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Application workload | Same pinned Pod-to-Pod tests | Same | Same |
| Pod/inner IP MTU | 1450 | 1450 | 1450 |
| Physical transport MTU | 1500 | 1500 | 1500 |
| Guest experiment NIC MTU | 1500 | 1500 | 1450 |
| Cilium mode | Tunnel | Native | Native |
| Pod endpoint BPF processing | Present | Present | Present |
| Tunnel selection | Cilium BPF/metadata path | Guest Linux routes and static entries | Host bridge and forwarding entries |
| Linux VXLAN execution | Guest kernel | Guest kernel | Hypervisor kernel |
| VM host-side attachment | Direct/macvtap | Direct/macvtap | TAP-backed Linux bridge |
| Packet at virtual NIC boundary | VXLAN-encapsulated | VXLAN-encapsulated | Inner frame |
| VXLAN UDP destination port | 8472 | 4789 | 4789 |
| Guest host-network control | Bypasses Pod overlay | Bypasses custom Pod tunnel | Traverses shared host tunnel |

Equal inner MTU removes one important packet-size difference, but the guest NIC MTU difference is intentional: guest encapsulation needs space for the added outer headers. Setting every interface to 1450 would introduce fragmentation or force a smaller usable inner MTU. Similarly, identical physical NIC offload settings do not prove identical effective offload behavior for packets arriving from different virtual interfaces and tunnel paths. UDP destination ports are another potential interaction with hardware tunnel recognition and hashing. The matched comparison must therefore retain the configuration and interface evidence, rather than assuming equal MTU establishes an identical path.

Host-network measurements also require care. Dividing Pod throughput by host-network throughput does not automatically normalize all three configurations: the denominator bypasses the overlay for guest-based tunnels but traverses it for the hypervisor tunnel. Pod-only tests provide the primary comparable workload across the three implementations. Host-network tests remain useful controls within each configuration, provided their different paths are stated explicitly.

### Stronger controls and improvements to kernel paths

The additional `bridge-1450` control implements native routing at Pod and guest MTU 1450 with a bridge-backed guest attachment and no VXLAN, paired with the existing direct/macvtap native baseline. Its physical uplink uses MTU 1500. It helps assess the effect of changing the attachment path before attributing a host-VXLAN difference to tunnel location; setup and validation are described in the [bottleneck study guide](docs/experiments/bottleneck-study.md). An even more controlled placement study could use a common bridge-backed attachment for both guest and host tunnels, but that would require new configurations and validation. Neither control guarantees an additive decomposition: removing a bridge or tunnel can change batching, offloads, and the location of the bottleneck, so throughput differences should not be subtracted and labeled isolated bridge or encapsulation costs.

Within the current design, retain native-1450 as the no-tunnel reference, treat Cilium versus custom guest VXLAN as the closest implementation comparison, and describe custom guest versus host VXLAN as a deployment-placement comparison. Verify actual traffic using targeted captures and counter deltas on guest interfaces, tunnel devices, host bridge/backend devices, and physical NICs. Use captures in separate validation runs where possible to limit measurement overhead. Record offload capabilities, queue distribution, CPU placement, and Hubble settings consistently; examine both single-stream and multistream behavior. Independent repeated rounds can assess stability but cannot remove structural differences between paths.

Kernel optimizations should be evaluated separately from tunnel placement. In the guests, BPF host routing can shorten regular host-stack traversal, while netkit can replace the veth-based Pod interface path. Cilium documents prerequisites for these features, including kube-proxy replacement and BPF masquerading for the recommended host-routing configuration, with netkit requiring BPF host routing and a suitable kernel. A comparison should hold those prerequisites constant when toggling legacy versus BPF host routing, then hold BPF routing constant when comparing veth versus netkit. These are proposed additional profiles, not features enabled in the current baseline. [Cilium's tuning guide](https://docs.cilium.io/en/stable/operations/performance/tuning/) describes the mechanisms and operational checks. Guest improvements do not eliminate hypervisor networking or virtual NIC processing. A separate custom hypervisor TC/BPF forwarding shortcut could investigate bridge-forwarding cost while retaining Linux VXLAN, but it would introduce another path that needs its own baseline, policy checks, and validation.

The defensible conclusion from the existing suite is therefore about end-to-end configurations: under matching workload, placement, and inner MTU, one deployment may provide different throughput, latency, and resource distribution from another. Attribution to a particular kernel stage requires the additional controls and profiling described above. A steady-state netperf benchmark also does not measure tunnel provisioning effort, control-plane convergence, or per-VM management overhead. Those require separate experiments involving configuration changes, failures, or increasing numbers of VMs. Making these boundaries explicit preserves the usefulness of the comparison while avoiding a claim that the current paths isolate a single implementation choice.
